\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) have established themselves as the state of the art for novel view synthesis in both academia and industry, enabling high-fidelity reconstruction of three-dimensional scenes from multi-view observations \cite{kerbl3Dgaussians, Tancik2023, substance3dviewer}. Nevertheless, most neural rendering techniques require substantial computational resources and densely sampled multi-view training data, which restricts their applicability to resource-constrained scenarios such as mobile scanning, virtual try-on systems, and sparse-view reconstruction.

Recent advances in neural rendering have focused on improving the computational efficiency and generalization capability of NeRF-based methods. Representative approaches include Instant-NGP (\textit{Instant Neural Graphics Primitives}), Nerfacto, PixelNeRF, and GNT (\textit{Generalizable NeRF Transformer}) \cite{muller2022, Tancik2023, yu2021pixelnerfneuralradiancefields, t2023attentionnerfneeds}. These methods explore different design strategies, including efficient spatial \textit{encoding} mechanisms \cite{muller2022}, hybrid architectures that integrate Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) \cite{schwarz2021grafgenerativeradiancefields, wu2021cvt, t2023attentionnerfneeds}, and model pre-computation (\textit{baking}) into compact representations with reduced memory requirements \cite{hedman2021bakingneuralradiancefields}. Although these developments have significantly improved different aspects of neural rendering, their computational characteristics and reconstruction performance have generally been evaluated independently or under different experimental settings.

Consequently, a systematic and reproducible comparison of generalizable and per-scene neural rendering architectures under standardized sparse-view protocols remains unavailable. Existing studies have primarily investigated NeRF models in limited-data scenarios \cite{niemeyer2022regnerf, kim2023poserefinement}, whereas benchmarking frameworks such as NerfBaselines \cite{kulhanek2025nerfbaselinesconsistentreproducibleevaluation} focus predominantly on traditional and per-scene methods. This hinders a comprehensive characterization of the trade-offs between computational efficiency and reconstruction fidelity across different neural rendering paradigms.

To address this limitation, we propose a systematic evaluation framework for neural rendering architectures under sparse-view conditions. Reconstruction fidelity is quantified using three widely adopted image quality metrics, namely PSNR (\textit{Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio}), SSIM (\textit{Structural Similarity Index Measure}) \cite{wang2004image}, and LPIPS (\textit{Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity}) \cite{zhang2018unreasonable}. Computational performance is quantified through complementary system-level metrics, including training convergence time, inference latency, and VRAM (\textit{Video Random Access Memory}) consumption, enabling a quantitative characterization of the trade-offs between computational cost and reconstruction quality. Thus, the main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize} 

\item A unified evaluation protocol was defined for generalizable and per-scene NeRF architectures, ensuring reproducibility through standardized data partitions, controlled execution environments, and consistent evaluation metrics across three sparse-view data regimes.

\item Integration wrappers extending the Nerfstudio framework with PixelNeRF and GNT were implemented and made publicly available, providing a unified experimental infrastructure for standardized benchmarking of heterogeneous neural rendering architectures. 

\end{itemize}
