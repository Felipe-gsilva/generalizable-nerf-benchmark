\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}


This section presents the proposed methodology for systematically evaluating the trade-offs between computational performance and reconstruction fidelity of NeRF models under sparse-view conditions. Figure \ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates the proposed evaluation pipeline, which consists of three main stages: (i) dataset selection and sparse-view sampling; (ii) configuration and training of the selected neural rendering architectures according to their respective learning paradigms; and (iii) quantitative evaluation using standardized metrics to jointly characterize computational efficiency and reconstruction fidelity.

\subsection{Dataset \& Splits}
\label{sec:data}
The experiments were conducted on the Local Light Field Fusion (LLFF) dataset, which comprises 8 real-world scenes captured with a forward-facing moving camera. Camera poses and depth bounds were obtained through COLMAP, following standard dataset procedures \cite{mildenhall2019locallightfieldfusion}. Furthermore, images were evaluated using downscale of 8 resolution ($504 \times 378$).

The complete data splitting protocol follows the few-shot evaluation configuration established by Niemeyer et al. \cite{niemeyer2022regnerf}. Specifically, the testing split was generated by reserving images with indices that are multiples of 8 (llffhold=8), while the training views for the sparse regimes were strictly limited to $N \in \{3, 6, 10\}$. The selection of these training views was performed using two distinct strategies: (i) uniform, where views are sampled via linear interpolation over the dataset indices, distributing them evenly along the camera trajectory (a strategy that does not generalize to other datasets, since LLFF indices encode camera trajectory order); and (ii) random, where views are randomly selected using a fixed seed (42) to ensure reproducibility. Comparing these two strategies assesses the models sensitivity to the spatial distribution of the input views, as demonstrated in Table \ref{tab:split}. Given the empirical advantage of uniform sampling (+0.54 dB PSNR on the fern scene), this strategy was adopted for all subsequent experiments.

Specific pipeline configurations were strictly enforced to guarantee stability and fair comparison. Notably, camera pose refinement was disabled for Nerfacto to keep all models on equal footing, as this feature is not natively supported by the other evaluated architectures. To minimize memory footprint and accelerate fine-tuning, mixed-precision training was enabled across all models. For hash-based models (Instant-NGP and Nerfacto), the multiscale hash table capacity was constrained to $2^{16}$, with backgrounds set to white. Structural hyperparameters for GNT were locked to 48 coarse and importance samples per ray, a transformer depth of 8, and a network width of 64, following its original configuration \cite{t2023attentionnerfneeds}.

\subsection{Models \& Training Regimes}
\label{sec:regimes}
The evaluation encompasses distinct architectural paradigms: generalizable models (PixelNeRF \cite{yu2021pixelnerfneuralradiancefields} and GNT \cite{t2023attentionnerfneeds}) and highly efficient per-scene baselines (Instant-NGP \cite{muller2022} and Nerfacto \cite{Tancik2023}).

Models were evaluated across three foundational regimes: (i) ZS (Zero-Shot): immediate inference utilizing pre-trained weights without any adaptation; (ii) FT (Fine-Tuning): optimizing pre-trained generalizable weights directly on sparse target views ($N \in \{3, 6, 10\}$); and (iii) Per-Scene: optimizing baseline models from scratch under identical view constraints. 

\subsubsection{Fine-Tuning Strategies and Implementation}
While Section \ref{sec:related_work} establishes the theoretical principles of fine-tuning regimes, their realization in generalizable NeRFs necessitates dedicated architectural hooks and parameter management logic. We implemented the five fine-tuning strategies within our custom Nerfstudio wrapper packages (\texttt{nerfstudio-gnt} and \texttt{nerfstudio-pixelnerf}) via a unified adaptation module (\texttt{adaptation.py}), decoupling strategy dispatch from the underlying volumetric rendering graph.

Parameter isolation is enforced dynamically at model initialization through \texttt{setup\_adaptation()} by configuring the \texttt{requires\_grad} property of sub-network parameter tensors and substituting targeted projection layers, as formalized in Algorithm \ref{alg:adaptation}. In Full Fine-Tuning (\textit{Full-FT}), gradient propagation is enabled uniformly across all parameters of the 2D feature extractor and the volumetric rendering network. In Partial Fine-Tuning of the Decoder (\textit{PFT-Decoder}), the spatial extractor (\texttt{feature\_net} in GNT, \texttt{encoder} in PixelNeRF) has all parameters frozen (\texttt{p.requires\_grad = False}) and is pinned to evaluation mode (\texttt{.eval()}), confining updates strictly to the rendering network (\texttt{net\_coarse} and \texttt{net\_fine} transformers in GNT, \texttt{mlp\_coarse} and \texttt{mlp\_fine} in PixelNeRF). In dissonance with the other investigated approaches, Partial Fine-Tuning of the Backbone (\textit{PFT-Backbone}) inverts this configuration by granting gradients solely to the 2D extractor while freezing all rendering modules.

Selective PEFT (\textit{BitFit}) freezes all multi-dimensional weight tensors throughout the network, deploying \texttt{apply\_bitfit()} to recursively isolate and activate gradient computation exclusively on one-dimensional additive bias vectors (\texttt{name.endswith(".bias")}). This restricts optimization to under $0.15\%$ of total network capacity without structural alterations. Additive PEFT (\textit{PEFT-LoRA}) freezes the base model entirely, after which \texttt{apply\_lora\_to\_module()} traverses the volumetric synthesis network to dynamically hot-swap linear projections (\texttt{q\_proj}, \texttt{v\_proj}, and dense MLP layers) with custom \texttt{LoRALinear} modules. Each adapter injects trainable down-projection $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}$ (initialized via Kaiming uniform) and up-projection $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}$ (initialized to zero) tensors, scaled by $\alpha / r$.

Integrating these selective updates with Nerfstudio's multi-group optimizer engine required resolving empty-gradient exceptions. Nerfstudio queries \texttt{get\_param\_groups()} to register distinct learning rates for the 2D extractor and volumetric networks. When a sub-network is completely frozen, passing empty parameter lists causes standard PyTorch optimizers to fail. Our wrapper dynamically inspects each group and substitutes an inert scalar parameter (\texttt{dummy\_param}) with \texttt{requires\_grad = False} whenever no trainable tensors exist, preventing optimization crashes. Furthermore, to prevent PyTorch's top-level \texttt{model.train()} toggle from reinstating batch normalization drift in frozen extractors, \texttt{train()} is overridden to maintain \texttt{.eval()} on inactive submodules. Finally, to eliminate runtime latency during evaluation, \texttt{merge\_lora\_weights()} folds the accumulated low-rank update $\Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r} BA$ directly into the base weights $W_0$ in-place prior to test-view synthesis, preserving native inference speed.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Dynamic Parameter Isolation and Fine-Tuning Setup}
\label{alg:adaptation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Pre-trained model $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{F}_{\text{enc}}, \mathcal{R}_{\text{dec}})$ with parameters $\Theta = \Theta_{\text{enc}} \cup \Theta_{\text{dec}}$, strategy $S$, LoRA rank $r$, scaling $\alpha$, target modules $\mathcal{T}$
\STATE Freeze all model parameters: $\nabla_{\theta} \leftarrow \text{False}, \forall \theta \in \Theta$
\IF{$S = \text{Zero-Shot}$}
    \STATE Set $\mathcal{F}_{\text{enc}}.\text{eval}()$ \COMMENT{No gradient updates}
\ELSIF{$S = \text{Full-FT}$}
    \STATE $\nabla_{\theta} \leftarrow \text{True}, \forall \theta \in (\Theta_{\text{enc}} \cup \Theta_{\text{dec}})$
\ELSIF{$S = \text{PFT-Decoder}$}
    \STATE Set $\mathcal{F}_{\text{enc}}.\text{eval}()$ and keep $\Theta_{\text{enc}}$ frozen
    \STATE $\nabla_{\theta} \leftarrow \text{True}, \forall \theta \in \Theta_{\text{dec}}$ \COMMENT{Tune rendering head only}
\ELSIF{$S = \text{PFT-Backbone}$}
    \STATE $\nabla_{\theta} \leftarrow \text{True}, \forall \theta \in \Theta_{\text{enc}}$ \COMMENT{Tune 2D spatial backbone only}
    \STATE Keep $\Theta_{\text{dec}}$ frozen
\ELSIF{$S = \text{BitFit}$}
    \FORALL{$\theta \in \Theta$}
        \IF{$\text{is\_bias}(\theta)$}
            \STATE $\nabla_{\theta} \leftarrow \text{True}$ \COMMENT{Activate 1D additive bias terms}
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\ELSIF{$S = \text{PEFT-LoRA}$}
    \STATE Set $\mathcal{F}_{\text{enc}}.\text{eval}()$
    \FORALL{$W_0 \in \mathcal{R}_{\text{dec}}$ matching $\mathcal{T}$}
        \STATE Replace $W_0$ with adapter: $W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r}(x A^T) B^T$
        \STATE Initialize $A \sim \mathcal{U}, B \leftarrow \mathbf{0}$
    \ENDFOR
\ENDIF
\STATE Filter active parameter groups: $\mathcal{G} \leftarrow \text{FilterTrainable}(\Theta, \theta_{\text{dummy}})$
\ENSURE Configured model $\mathcal{M}$ and optimizer parameter groups $\mathcal{G}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Stratified K-Fold Grid Search Protocol}
Hyperparameter optimization and seed robustness were assessed using a Stratified $K$-Fold cross-validation protocol ($K=4$) across all eight LLFF scenes. Each fold paired a high-frequency foliage scene with a structured architectural capture: Fold 0 (\textit{fern}, \textit{fortress}), Fold 1 (\textit{flower}, \textit{horns}), Fold 2 (\textit{leaves}, \textit{room}), and Fold 3 (\textit{orchids}, \textit{trex}). For every adaptation strategy, the search grid spanned three learning rate magnitudes $\eta \in \{10^{-4}, 5\times 10^{-4}, 10^{-3}\}$ and two LoRA ranks $r \in \{4, 8\}$ (with $\alpha = 2r$), replicated across three independent random seeds $\{42, 123, 999\}$ under $N=3$ input views.

To establish a calibrated iteration budget for this grid search, an empirical ablation over fine-tuning steps was conducted across the range $\{0, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500\}$, as depicted in Figure \ref{fig:ft_steps_ablation}. As observed in Figure \ref{fig:ft_steps_ablation}(a), PixelNeRF under \textit{PFT-Backbone} rapidly ascends from $5.70$ dB to peak visual quality at exactly $500$ steps ($11.03$ dB PSNR, $0.150$ SSIM). Beyond $500$ steps, the model undergoes pronounced overfitting under the sparse three-view supervision, degrading to $8.57$ dB at $2500$ steps with a corresponding rise in perceptual loss (Figure \ref{fig:ft_steps_ablation}(c)). For GNT under \textit{PEFT-LoRA}, optimization advances monotonically, capturing the majority of its adaptation gain ($14.29$ dB, $+4.10$ dB over zero-shot) within the first $500$ steps before plateauing towards $14.99$ dB at $2000$ steps. Consequently, $500$ fine-tuning steps was selected as the rigorous standard for all subsequent grid search experiments, maximizing PixelNeRF's fidelity prior to geometric breakdown while capturing over $90\%$ of GNT's asymptotic performance within an efficient computational window. Optimization runs encountering numerical breakdown due to dense occlusion geometry (yielding 0.0 dB PSNR on \textit{leaves} and \textit{orchids}) were systematically pruned to prevent distorting aggregate statistics.

Standardizing the experimental framework required integrating all evaluated models into the Nerfstudio platform \cite{Tancik2023}. Although Instant-NGP and Nerfacto are natively supported, PixelNeRF and GNT required custom wrapper implementations, which we developed and released publicly. These modules map each model's native inference logic directly to Nerfstudio's ray generators and field architectures, incorporating tiny-cuda-nn \cite{tinycudann} bindings for hardware acceleration.

Training used the Adam optimizer without weight decay, processing mini-batches of 512 rays during training and 128 rays during evaluation. Preliminary regime baselines ran for 2,500 iterations, the multi-seed fine-tuning grid search executed for 500 steps, and per-scene models were trained from scratch to convergence over 30,000 iterations. Although per-architecture tuning of $\eta$ could yield higher peak metrics (Table \ref{tab:ablation_lr}), systematic grid search was conducted to map optimal learning rates per strategy.

The complete wrapper codebase is available at \url{https://github.com/Felipe-gsilva/Few-Shot-NeRFs/} and distributed as standalone packages via PyPI and PiWheels (\url{https://www.piwheels.org/project/nerfstudio-gnt/} and \url{https://www.piwheels.org/project/nerfstudio-pixelnerf/}). Benchmark scripts, configurations, and raw experimental logs are hosted at \url{https://github.com/Felipe-gsilva/nerf-ann-paper/}.

\subsection{Evaluation \& Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The evaluation protocol assesses three complementary performance dimensions: visual fidelity, operational speed, and memory consumption. All reported metrics derive exclusively from the held-out test views (Section \ref{sec:data}). Visual reconstruction quality is quantified through Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) \cite{wang2004image}, and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) \cite{zhang2018unreasonable}. In accordance with Niemeyer et al. \cite{niemeyer2022regnerf}, LPIPS employs VGG feature extractors to evaluate perceptual distance. Computational efficiency is assessed via rendering throughput in frames per second (FPS) over the test views \cite{kulhanek2025nerfbaselinesconsistentreproducibleevaluation}. Memory demands are characterized by static model parameter footprints alongside peak dynamic VRAM allocation recorded by PyTorch during inference. Execution within Nvidia Docker containers guaranteed environmental isolation and reproducible hardware telemetry on an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1660 Super GPU (6GB VRAM, CUDA 12.6).
